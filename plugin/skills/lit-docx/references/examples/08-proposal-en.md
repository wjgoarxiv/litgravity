# Proposal in brief

Nearly four in ten children in the south district arrive at school by car, and the streets around the gates are at their most crowded in the same quarter hour in which the youngest pupils are being dropped. A two-week trial in March 2026 at one school, Hillside Primary (an example school), put two volunteers at the head and tail of a line of 14 children on one route. By the second week 11 of those children walked every day, three parents who used to drive stopped, and the head teacher reported fewer cars on the access road. This proposal turns that single route into a district scheme: four routes at each of six schools, each led by two trained volunteers, running every school morning.

::: keyfigures
- **24** walking routes (sample)
  - Four routes at each of six schools, running from February 2027
- **About 290** pupils walking in a group each day (sample)
  - Twelve children a route on average, a little under a quarter of all pupils
- **170** fewer car arrivals each morning (sample)
  - Six in ten of the walking pupils would otherwise arrive by car
:::

The trust asks the district's travel fund for 74,000 over twelve months. The money pays for a part-time coordinator, volunteer training, safety equipment, insurance, route assessments and an evaluation at the end of the pilot. The schools contribute the use of a room for training and a noticeboard at the gate at no cost.

# Need and evidence

## What parents told us

A short survey went home with the pupils in May 2026. Of 412 parents who answered, 64 % said they would let their child walk to school in a supervised group, and 52 % named traffic around the gate as the main reason they chose to drive. A further 23 % said they drive because the walk is too long to do alone, which a group with an adult at each end addresses directly.

| Car | 471 | 38 |

## Why the schools

All six schools sit within a mile of their pupils' homes, five have a quiet side street that can serve as a collection point, and each head teacher has agreed to host the scheme as part of the school travel plan. The schools place the routes on their noticeboards and newsletters, which gives the scheme a channel to every family at no cost.

Four partners take part. The trust runs the pilot and employs the coordinator; the schools host the routes and recruit parent ambassadors; about 60 volunteer leaders, checked and trained in child safety, walk the routes; and the council's road safety team assesses each route before it opens and counts cars at the gate before and after, so that the reduction reported at the end uses the council's own count method.

# Budget and plan

## Budget

The request covers twelve months of running costs. A third of it pays the part-time coordinator who keeps the leader rota, the route calendar and the attendance log; the rest buys leader training and equipment, insures the walks and pays for the route assessments, volunteer expenses and the external evaluation. The schools give the rooms at no cost, so no rent appears in the table.

## How a route runs

Each route has a fixed start at 08:05 and reaches the school gate by 08:40. Children join at marked stops along the way and sign in with the leader at the head, who carries the register. A second leader walks at the tail with the first aid kit. A route needs a minimum of five children to run, and a route that stays below that for a month is merged with its neighbour. Leaders walk the route once without children before the launch so that crossings, stops and timings are fixed.

::: columns 2
### Months 1 to 3

Appoint the coordinator, assess the 24 routes with the council, recruit and check the first 30 leaders, and fit out three schools.

### Months 4 to 6

Launch 12 routes at three schools in the spring term, add a second training round, and start the monthly gate counts with the road safety team.

<!-- column-break -->

### Months 7 to 9

Launch the other 12 routes at the remaining schools, add a wet-weather plan, and train six leaders as route captains.

### Months 10 to 12

Carry out the evaluation, publish the results, and agree with each school how the routes continue under its travel plan without project funding.
:::

# Outcomes and evaluation

The pilot will count children, count cars at the gate and survey families at every school. The external evaluation will compare the cost per walking pupil with the cost of the traffic measures the district already uses outside school gates, and will report what children and leaders say they gained from the walk.

Table 3. Pilot measures and how they are counted (sample data)

| Measure | Expected over the pilot | Counted by |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Routes running | 24 | Coordinator's route calendar |
| Pupils walking in a group each day | 290 | Leader's register |
| Car arrivals avoided each morning | 170 | Council gate counts |
| Trained volunteer leaders | 60 | Training records |
| Funding per walking pupil | 255 | Total request over pupils walking |

Source: Example Neighbourhood Trust pilot plan (sample)

Besides the cars removed from the gate, the evaluation will set the funding per walking pupil, about 255, against the cost of a crossing patrol or a school street closure, which for a school of this size is usually higher.

The main risk is leader turnover. The plan trains more leaders than the routes need on any one morning and names six route captains by month nine, so that the scheme does not depend on the coordinator alone when the funding ends.

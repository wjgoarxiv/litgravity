# Background

Toilet flushing is the largest single use of mains water in a primary school, and in the trial district it takes about a third of the annual bill. The roofs above those toilets shed far more rain each year than the flushing needs. Collecting roof water in a tank and piping it to the cisterns therefore looks like a tidy match of supply and demand. The question this trial set out to answer was a practical one: how much of the flushing demand a school tank can meet through a full school year, and which inexpensive additions keep the system dependable enough that caretakers trust it.

Eight schools took part between September 2024 and July 2026. They ranged from 140 to 420 pupils, and all had flat bitumen roofs built between 1958 and 1979. Four received a tank, pump and cistern connection with no other work (group A). Four received the same installation together with three low-cost additions: a first-flush diverter, a mains top-up valve set to refill the tank only when it falls to a fifth full, and a level gauge fixed on the corridor wall (group B). Each school kept its mains supply to the cisterns as a standing backup.

The groups were matched on pupil numbers and roof area rather than drawn at random. The estate unit paired schools of similar size, and the trial team assigned one of each pair to group B by coin toss, which kept the groups close in demand, roof area and exposure to wind.

## Measurement approach

Each school had a meter on the tank outlet, a meter on the mains top-up line and a weekly water sample taken at the cistern supply. The rainwater share is the volume drawn from the tank divided by the total volume flushed. A school with a share of 50 % therefore pays mains rates for half of its flushing water. Quality was judged by the share of weekly samples that stayed under a turbidity of 1 NTU and showed no E. coli.

Three further terms recur below. The first-flush diverter sends the first millimetres of each rainfall, which carry roof dirt, to the drain before the tank fills. The top-up valve is the mains refill that keeps cisterns working when the tank runs dry. Empty days are the days in a school year on which the tank held less than a tenth of its volume at 08:00, so that the mains supply carried the morning flush.

# Capture results

## Two school years compared

Group B drew more of its flushing water from the tank in both years. In the first year group A met 41 % of flushing demand from rain and group B met 63 %. The gap had two sources. In group A the top-up valve refilled the tank from the mains the moment the level dropped, so the stored space was taken before rain could use it. And when cloudy samples appeared, caretakers in group A often closed the tank supply and flushed from the mains until the weekly result came back clean. In the second year, after group A reset its valves to the group B setting at no cost, its share rose only to 44 % while group B held at 66 %, which puts the larger part of the gap on dirt and upkeep rather than on the valve.

Empty days tell the same story from the other side. Group A's tanks stood empty on 96 days in the first year, mostly in the three weeks after each school holiday, when the roof had shed nothing and pupils were flushing at full rate. Resetting the valves cut that figure only slightly, because a dry roof gives the tank nothing to store whatever the valve does. In group B the tanks ran dry for 38 days and then 33, which is close to the days the roof stood in frost.

## Installation and operation

Each tank was sized from the measured flushing demand of the school in the term before the trial, not from the roof area, which in six of the eight schools would have produced a tank twice as large as the cisterns could use. Installation took between five and nine working days per school, with the toilets out of service for no more than one morning. Two of the eight systems needed a return visit in the first month, both for a pump pressure setting rather than a fault.

Smell and noise were the concerns caretakers raised most often before installation. The pumps sit in plant rooms away from classrooms, and after the first month no school recorded more than one complaint per term. Stored water that sits through the summer holiday can turn stale, which showed as a few weeks of cloudy samples in September but did not reach the cisterns once the first-flush diverters were in place.

# Quality and cost

## The weakest weeks

Average figures hide the weeks that mattered. In group A the samples taken in the first two weeks after each holiday failed the turbidity limit in almost one case in five, and caretakers there were the ones who most often asked for the mains supply to be switched back on. In group B the diverter sent the first dirty water to the drain, and the same weeks failed in fewer than one case in twenty.

The summer term was the weakest for both groups. The warm weather brings fewer, heavier storms, and the long dry spells before them empty the tanks. Group B held up better because its top-up valve left the tank room to catch a storm instead of refilling to the brim.

## Running costs for the school

At the water and sewerage rate of 4.80 per cubic metre in the second year (sample figure), a group A school saved about 1,270 a year against flushing from the mains alone, and a group B school saved about 1,900. The difference between the groups follows the efficiency of the tanks; the tariff was the same, and rainfall in the two years was within 4 % of the district average.

# School experience

## Caretaker time and consent

Time was the scarce resource. Each tank needed the mesh filter cleaned every six weeks and the diverter chamber emptied each term, which caretakers put at about two hours a month. In the first two group B schools the work was not on any rota and slipped, and two weeks of cloudy samples followed. The trial team then changed its approach: a laminated card by the plant-room door, one cleaning date per month entered in the school calendar, and a call from the installer after the first cleaning.

Head teachers agreed to the works quickly, because nothing was built inside classrooms. No school asked to leave the trial, and two group A schools asked to have the group B additions fitted after reading the first-year results.

## Pupils and the level gauge

The level gauge in group B corridors drew more attention than any other part of the installation. Teachers in three of the four schools used it for weekly lessons on rainfall and water use, and pupils began to track the level after a dry week. The mains bills did not change in presentation, since the school still pays one water account, but the trial added a notice beside each gauge showing the mains water saved to date. Pupils flushed no less often in the schools with a gauge, and the metered flushing volume did not drop, which suggests the lessons raised awareness without cutting use.

# Discussion

The trial supports a modest conclusion. A tank alone can meet part of a school's flushing demand, but it then relies on mains refills at the wrong moments, empties early in dry spells and delivers a lower share than the roof area suggests. Three inexpensive additions, chosen by watching which weeks the tanks run dry, close most of that gap. None of the group B additions needed work inside a classroom, which mattered for consent: head teachers accepted a plant-room installation far more readily than a rebuild of the toilet block.

Two limits apply. All eight roofs were flat bitumen, so the results may not carry over to pitched tiled roofs, which shed rain faster and carry different dirt. And two school years is a short record for a pump rated for fifteen years; the replacement cost of pumps and filters is not yet known with any confidence. On water bills alone, a group A installation at a median of 21,500 repays itself in about 17 years, which is beyond the life of its pump.

A third question sits outside the schools. Three of the tanks overflowed to the street drain on the wettest days, and in one case the connection had to be enlarged because it backed up into the yard. A district programme would plan the overflow route street by street, as part of the drainage work, rather than school by school as in the trial.

::: callout kind=note title="What the trial did not test"
Rainwater was used for toilet flushing only. Hand-washing, kitchens and irrigation were left out on purpose, since each would need treatment and testing that the schools could not fund within the trial period.
:::

# Recommendations

For schools planning similar installations, the trial points to a sequence. First, meter the toilet flushing for a term to size the tank on measured demand. Second, set the mains top-up valve to refill at a fifth full, which costs almost nothing and in group A alone lifted the rainwater share by three points, from 41 % to 44 %. Third, fit a first-flush diverter and a mesh filter where the roof is dirty, and add the level gauge so pupils and caretakers can see the tank. Only then connect the tank to the cisterns, keeping the mains supply live as a backup throughout.

These additions cost about a seventh of the tank installation, whose median was 21,500 per school. At the second-year tariff they repay themselves in under five years, through the extra 630 or so saved each year compared with group A. Spent when the tank is first installed, they also make a smaller tank adequate, which recovers part of the cost at the outset.

# Further work

The meters stay in place for a third school year, which will give the first maintenance figures for pumps and filters and a longer record of empty days. A second trial in four schools with pitched tiled roofs is planned for 2027, using the same groups and additions so that the two roof types can be compared directly. The estate unit will also publish its sizing method, so that schools can fit a tank to their measured flushing demand before they commission any design work.
